# Active Learning — German for real life

Research and curriculum design • 13 September 2026 • Proposed course, not yet implemented or teacher-validated

## Decision

Create a separate **Active Learning** section with an A1 → A2 → B1 communication course. Organise it by things learners can accomplish with people, not by a requirement to demonstrate a grammar rule. Retain the existing course as a source of stories, vocabulary and grammar support. Do not clone its 72 assignments or replace the current speaking drill with a complicated mission dashboard.

The promise should be: **“Build the German to handle everyday life, one conversation and message at a time.”** The target is independent B1 performance in familiar real-life situations, not error-free speech, native-like fluency, university-level academic German, or a guaranteed result from completing a checklist.

Research informs this design; the module count, lesson sizes, retry rules and review intervals below are design choices to test. They are not CEFR requirements or experimentally established optimal settings.

## What the evidence changes

| Finding | Design consequence | Limit of the evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CEFR includes reception, production, interaction and mediation across personal, public, educational and professional domains. | Teach asking, responding, clarifying and passing on information, alongside writing and short talks. | CEFR gives descriptors, not a mandated grammar order or fixed number of lessons. [Council of Europe](https://www.coe.int/en/web/portfolio/the-common-european-framework-of-reference-%20for-languages-learning-teaching-assessment-cefr-) |
| A1 permits very simple communication with a supportive partner; A2 centres on routine exchanges; B1 includes connected accounts and familiar spontaneous interaction. | Begin with a single meaningful phrase, then combine ideas, then handle a follow-up or a change of plan. | A polished prepared monologue does not establish spontaneous interaction. [CEFR self-assessment](https://europass.europa.eu/en/common-european-framework-reference-language-skills) |
| Classroom oral corrective feedback can support learning; prompt-based feedback has shown advantages over recasts in some comparisons. | Give a correction with a short explanation; ask for one manageable retry, and make hints available. | These are classroom findings, not proof that our AI feedback is correct or equally effective. [Lyster & Saito](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263109990520) |
| A Bayesian synthesis of controlled written-feedback studies reports a durable moderate effect on accuracy, with comparable effects across several feedback types. | Provide meaning-preserving corrections and explanations; avoid claiming one feedback format is universally best. | Accuracy improvement is narrower than overall communicative ability; studies differ substantially. [Brown, Liu & Norouzian](https://doi.org/10.1177/13621688221147374) |
| Spaced L2 practice generally outperforms massed practice; effects depend on task and retention interval. | Revisit a construction later instead of requiring many consecutive identical retries. | Our proposed 1–3, 7+ and 21+ day windows are scheduling hypotheses, not a universal optimum. [Kim & Webb](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12479) |
| Task repetition can improve rehearsed performance without proving durable transfer to another prompt. | Use a fresh situation, changed details and an unprepared follow-up for progress evidence. | Changing one name alone is not a strong transfer test. [Suzuki & Hanzawa](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/massed-task-repetition-is-a-doubleedged-sword-for-fluency-development/D28EDD7E3D0FA15630165538D706E80F) |

Official Goethe samples provide useful external task references. A2 writing includes short messages and emails; B1 writing includes emails and an opinion contribution. B1 speaking includes collaborative planning, presentation and discussion. Borrow the communicative demands, not copyrighted prompts or an exam-only course structure. Exam task word counts are not a universal CEFR definition. [A2 writing](https://bfu.goethe.de/a2_mod_2MX5/schreiben.php), [B1 writing](https://bfu.goethe.de/b1_mod/schreiben.php), [B1 speaking](https://bfu.goethe.de/b1_mod/sprechen.php)

A short output course alone cannot credibly promise zero-to-B1. Goethe gives a broad orientation of 350–650 teaching units of 45 minutes to reach B1, dependent on the learner. This is not a personalised completion forecast. Active Learning must connect to substantial listening, reading, vocabulary and grammar practice and opportunities to communicate beyond the app. [Goethe B1 information](https://www.goethe.de/ins/de/de/m/prf/prf/gzb1/inf.html)

## Course size and daily workload

**36 modules: 12 per level. Each module contains four paired lessons.** Each pair has one speaking task and one writing task about the same function, with different details so the second is not simply copied. This gives 144 paired lessons / 288 core submissions. Each module also has a later speaking check and a later writing check: 72 additional check opportunities, or 360 planned activities before adaptive retries. These are activities, not consecutive days or a fluency guarantee.

A learner normally completes **one mode per visit**, not both. The other mode is the next suggested activity. The level map displays the next small step rather than a wall of 360 assignments. A returning learner takes a short placement sampler and can skip supported practice when unfamiliar tasks show readiness; speaking and writing placement may differ.

| Phase | Suggested visit | Writing scope | Speaking scope | Support |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A1 module 1 | 3–5 minutes | One phrase or sentence, then two | One short answer; no minimum seconds | Listenable example, meaning and optional starter |
| A1 modules 2–4 | 5–8 minutes | 2–3 simple sentences or fields | 1–2 short answers, one at a time | Small phrase bank; replay available |
| A1 modules 5–8 | 6–10 minutes | 3–4 simple sentences; short practical note | 2–3 brief exchanges | A small cue, then fading support |
| A1 modules 9–12 | 8–12 minutes | Usually 20–40 words where a message warrants it | Brief routine exchange with a supportive partner | Learner can ask for repetition |
| A2 modules 1–6 | 8–12 minutes | Roughly 30–60 words when appropriate | 3–4 exchanges; short description | Planning keywords; one supplied constraint |
| A2 modules 7–12 | 10–15 minutes | Roughly 50–80 words when appropriate | 4–5 exchanges including a clarification/change | Fewer cues; still familiar situations |
| B1 modules 1–6 | 12–18 minutes | Usually 70–110 words for connected tasks | 4–6 responsive exchanges or a short account | Optional planning, no compulsory script |
| B1 modules 7–12 | 15–20 minutes, resumable | Usually 80–150 words for longer tasks; functional messages may be shorter | Discussion, narration or negotiation with unprepared follow-up | No content hints in evidence checks; normal communication repair allowed |

Length is a workload guide, not the main success criterion. A complete short answer should not fail for lacking filler. Do not force an A1 learner to speak for a minute to say their name. Split a longer B1 task into planning and submission visits if needed.

## The first four paired lessons, fully specified

These are the first module's eight small activities, not eight requirements on day one. Default order is speaking 1, writing 1, speaking 2, writing 2, etc. Learners can choose the other mode.

| Lesson | Input and prerequisites | Speaking prompt | Writing prompt | What counts as success |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A1.01.1 — Say your name | Hear “Ich heiße Lina.” Meaning: “My name is Lina.” Teach this as a usable chunk; no conjugation chart. | “Wie heißt du?” English help: “Say your name.” | “Write your name as one German sentence for a new classmate.” | An understandable name introduction; “Ich bin …” is also valid. Name-only is acknowledged as a first step, followed by an optional sentence starter. |
| A1.01.2 — Say where you are from | Hear “Ich komme aus Indien.” Offer the learner's chosen country phrase, including any required article. | “Woher kommst du?” | “Send a new classmate one sentence saying which country you are from.” | Clear origin. Do not demand articles the lesson has not supplied; offer relevant phrasing such as “aus der Türkei”. |
| A1.01.3 — Say where you live | Hear “Ich wohne in Bonn.” Reuse ich + verb; teach wohnen as a chunk. | “Wo wohnst du?” | “Write your town or city in one sentence for a class introduction.” | Understandable present home location. A fictional city is allowed. No address required. |
| A1.01.4 — Put it together | Revisit the three short chunks, then hide the sample before the learner responds. | “Introduce yourself to a new classmate.” Ask name, origin and city separately if the learner needs help. | “Write a tiny introduction with your name and where you live. Add your origin if you want.” | Two understandable personal details. Support is recorded, not treated as independent evidence. |

First visit: one short model → one answer → one precise success statement → at most one correction → optional retry → “Done for today.” Example feedback for “Ich heißen Mira”: **“I understood your name. Say: Ich heiße Mira. With ich, use heiße. Try it once more.”** Do not introduce subordinate clauses, all verb endings, a score dashboard or pronunciation claims.

Later module check: a new person asks for the learner's name and location in a different order. Writing uses a fresh club profile rather than replaying the exact class prompt. Do not fail an A1 learner for using a legitimate memorised expression; record the degree of support accurately.

## What every lesson must contain

Before any lesson can be published, its authoring record must include: level and stage; communicative goal; audience and du/Sie register; required prior language; a tiny reading/listening input; 3–5 useful chunks at most for early A1; precise task; acceptable content alternatives; teacher-authored examples; a speaking follow-up policy; feedback priorities; likely recognition confusions; a retry variation; an unseen later-check variant; and a teacher-review status.

The accompanying `SYLLABUS.md` defines all 36 modules and the four lessons within each. It is a course-design syllabus, not a claim that all learner-facing scripts, recordings and validated rubrics have been authored. `lesson-map.csv` makes all 144 paired lessons individually addressable for implementation.

**Prerequisite rule:** before asking for a function, verify the learner has encountered its language in a short understandable input or an existing course lesson. Provide a 30–90 second bridge where needed. Do not simply stamp an A1/A2/B1 label on a hard task. The task bank must be independent of the existing chapter-number rotation.

**Increase one main difficulty at a time:** amount of information; less planning; less support; a new communicative purpose; register; or an unexpected response. Avoid simultaneously increasing all of them. If a learner repeatedly struggles, shorten the task and restore a cue. Do not trap them in endless identical retries.

## Tutor behaviour: detailed help without a wall of text

Keep the surface the user requested: **question → speak/type → feedback + correction → retry.** Show one question at a time even when later levels involve an exchange. No mode picker, mission setup, obligatory transcript confirmation or empty memory dashboard.

The first feedback view shows:

1. Did the intended message get across? One specific, truthful sentence.
2. The most useful correction, or at most two for later levels.
3. A brief explanation and a retry button.

“See detailed feedback” expands the rest: original versus meaning-preserving corrected version; exact changed spans; why each change is needed; error versus optional style; one small contrasting example; unanswered task points; and a suggested next practice item. Do not invent errors or inflate the learner's language to a level they could not reproduce. A full corrected form is available after submission, as requested, but copying it does not count as independent progress.

For short answers, corrected forms can be shown directly. For long text, preserve meaning, tone, names and factual details. If meaning is unclear, ask a specific clarification rather than rewriting a guess. Separate “more natural” suggestions from genuine mistakes. Do not present confidence percentages as certainty.

Feedback priorities: **A1** comprehensible message and one useful basic form; **A2** message completion, sequence and recurring errors; **B1** clarity, reasons, register, references and errors that impede meaning. An optional full analysis should not become a list of every stylistic preference.

A retry should make the learner do something: say the corrected sentence from memory; apply it to a different person or item; or rewrite one unclear sentence. After two unsuccessful retries, offer a simpler example and a shorter task, then a path forward. These caps are design defaults to test.

### Speaking requires interaction and listening

Static question-answer drills are the entry point, not the whole B1 course. Later tasks need an AI partner that responds to the learner's actual answer, asks for clarification, changes a relevant detail, disagrees politely or offers an alternative. Keep these behaviours inside teacher-authored scenario limits; do not let the AI jump to unrelated advanced topics.

Start with slow, replayable audio and visible meaning. Gradually fade the transcript, introduce familiar voices and natural pacing, and test comprehension of a new response. Always preserve accessibility options. Teach “Noch einmal, bitte”, “Langsamer, bitte”, “Meinen Sie …?” and asking for a missing word as communication skills, not failures.

Speech-to-text is fallible. Show “I heard…” with an optional correction path, not a compulsory extra step. Re-grade after a recognition correction; do not reinforce errors from a known bad transcript. Unconfirmed transcript-based feedback may help practice but cannot by itself certify spoken competence. Detailed pronunciation, accent or prosody scores require validated audio assessment; until then use teacher/listener review of sampled recordings with separate consent. Fluency evaluation needs timing/audio and responsiveness, not just a cleaned transcript.

## Confidence and return visits

Confidence should come from small real accomplishments, not exaggerated praise or an artificial “fluent” percentage. These are product hypotheses to evaluate, not demonstrated effects of this app.

- The first session ends after one useful utterance. “You introduced yourself” is a better result than an unexplained 92/100.
- Show a single suggested next task and a clear stopping point. Another activity is optional.
- Keep completed work after a missed day. No streak-loss punishment or public comparison.
- Permit fictional family, home and work details. Offer an equivalent friend/household example for sensitive topics.
- Revisit an earlier task after a few modules so the learner can notice reduced support or a clearer answer.
- Student and worker contexts branch when appropriate, but every learner encounters common school, college and office vocabulary. Do not force an adult to roleplay being a child.
- Ask occasionally “How ready would you feel using this with a real person?” Compare self-report with performance rather than treating it as a skill score.
- Offer safe optional real-world practice: ask a willing partner a simple question; draft a real message before sending it; rehearse an actual upcoming situation. Never send messages or collect recordings of other people automatically.

## Progress and assessment

Track writing and speaking separately. A strong writer may still need supported speaking. Record task completion, assistance, correction views, retries, recognition edits and delayed evidence independently. A completed lesson is not a mastered skill.

Proposed evidence states: **Practised → Used with support → Used independently → Used again later.** The learner view can show a simple “I can…” list; detailed evidence remains available for review. Asking a conversation partner to repeat a sentence is a legitimate CEFR communication strategy and is not equivalent to receiving the German answer from the tutor.

For each module, schedule two unfamiliar checks, one per mode, after a gap of at least seven days where feasible. Practice may continue while checks become due. Reuse key functions with a different setting and additional information; do not only swap a name. At roughly three weeks, sample earlier skills in a mixed task. These intervals are provisional.

Provisional rubric, rated separately on a 0–3 scale:

| Dimension | What the reviewer checks |
| --- | --- |
| Task success | Does the listener/reader have the information or action needed? |
| Comprehensibility | Can meaning be understood without reconstructing it? For speech, listen to audio where consent permits. |
| Level-appropriate language | Are taught words and structures sufficient and reasonably controlled for this stage? |
| Organisation | Can the reader/listener follow the sequence and references? A1 does not require sophisticated cohesion. |
| Interaction / register | Speaking: relevant response, question or repair. Writing: suitable tone and form for this recipient. |

Anchors: 0 = no usable evidence; 1 = substantial help or major breakdown; 2 = achieves the goal with level-appropriate limitations; 3 = achieves it clearly and flexibly for the target. Exact operational thresholds require teacher calibration. Do not average away a communication breakdown with good spelling.

Suggested module-readiness rule to pilot: task success and comprehensibility at least 2 in both modes, no answer-generating hints in the check, and success on a later unfamiliar variant. Gaps trigger targeted practice; they do not erase completed lessons or block all other topics. A final B1 readiness claim requires several tasks across everyday, educational/professional and public settings plus human review, not one AI score.

### Level-end assessment packs

| Level | Speaking sample | Writing sample | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A1 | Introduce self; ask for simple information; make a basic purchase/request with repetition allowed | Enter fictional personal details; write a brief familiar note | Simple supported everyday communication, not general independent conversation |
| A2 | Describe a recent event; organise a familiar plan; handle a routine change | Short message with reason/change; practical email with relevant details | Routine exchange, not prolonged argument or complex negotiation |
| B1 | Tell an experience; make and justify a shared plan; solve a familiar service problem; answer an unprepared follow-up | Connected personal account; practical formal request; short opinion with reasons | Functional independence on familiar matters, with hesitation and errors still possible |

Use multiple unseen variants, different partner voices, and teacher-scored audio/text. Report speaking and writing readiness separately. If no audio or human assessment exists, label the outcome “practice completed”, not “B1 fluency verified”.

## Build plan for the new section

This document is the research/design deliverable. The new section has not been built or published by this research step.

1. **Author and review A1 module 1 first.** Produce its eight tiny activities, audio examples, task criteria, corrections and two later-check variants. Test with real beginners before mass-authoring all levels.
2. **Add `/active-learning` with a single next task.** Include a compact level/module map and separate writing/speaking evidence. Keep existing course and progress intact. Use stable IDs such as `active-a1-m01-l01-speaking-v1`; never infer a task from array rotation.
3. **Reuse the safe foundations, extend the teaching model.** Existing transcription, photo confirmation, span validation, authentication and account quotas can be reused. Add input assets, prerequisite tags, task versions, retry variants, descriptor-scale references and evidence records. Do not make the client authoritative for task prompts or scoring criteria.
4. **Pilot the first three A1 modules.** Verify the first task is comprehensible, users can finish without technical help, and corrections do not overwhelm them. Then author and review the rest of A1.
5. **Add A2 interaction, then B1 interaction and connected writing.** Keep the single-question interface while introducing bounded adaptive responses. Add human audio checks before any oral-readiness claim.
6. **Run the delayed-outcome pilot before broad marketing.** Compare baseline and unfamiliar follow-up tasks with blinded teacher ratings. If feasible, compare the new course with the existing task drill under similar practice time.

Engineering requirements: preserve user history with versioned tasks; account-owned idempotent requests; durable daily budgets; bounded provider calls; save drafts and audio-check failure state; recognition correction/re-grading; shared error memory that excludes disputed/uncertain evidence; deletion of derived evidence when source work is deleted; explicit consent and retention rules. Stored audio must be opt-in and separately justified, not introduced silently for analytics.

## Validation and go/no-go checks

Beginner usability: observe 5–8 true beginners on the first activity before a larger trial. Language pilot: recruit 20–30 learners initially, stratifying reported/assessed level; this is exploratory and not a powered efficacy trial. Use independent German-teacher review, unfamiliar delayed tasks after 7+ days, and a further follow-up where feasible.

Measure: independent task completion; assistance needed; false corrections; major meaning changes; recognition error corrections; appropriate interaction; revision completion; recurring errors per opportunity; self-reported confidence; return rate; completion time; latency and cost per completed task. Report attrition and missing data. Engagement alone is not evidence of language improvement.

Before launch, require teacher approval of each released module's task difficulty and language examples; successful novice usability checks; no known meaning-changing or high-severity feedback defects in the release evaluation set; operational cost and latency within a chosen budget; and verified deletion/account-isolation behaviour. Numeric efficacy thresholds and sample sizes should be set before a larger controlled study, not invented after results are observed.

## Research notes and limitations

Sources were checked on 13 September 2026. Official CEFR/Goethe sources informed proficiency boundaries and task types. Research summaries above rely on publisher abstracts and accessible article text; they are not a systematic literature review and do not establish effects for German AI tutoring specifically. No source proves that exactly 12 modules, a particular word count, two retries or these review intervals will produce B1 competence. The curriculum is our design proposal; teacher review, learner evidence and revisions remain essential.
